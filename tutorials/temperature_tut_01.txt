# How LLM Temperature Actually Works — Explained Through a 10-Second Animation 🌡️
You’ve probably heard: **“Lower temperature makes an LLM more predictable; higher temperature makes it more creative.”**

But what actually happens inside the model?

This 10-second animation shows the mathematics behind temperature—and how it interacts with **top-p sampling**. Here’s how to follow it, even if you’re new to LLMs.

### 1. Start with the next-token prediction
An LLM generates text one **token** at a time. A token can be a word, part of a word, or punctuation.

For example, given:

**“I drink a cup of…”**

The model scores possible next tokens such as “tea” and “coffee.”

At the beginning of the video, you see a simplified flow:

**Input → Transformer / hidden states → Output scores**

The transformer processes the context and produces a hidden-state vector—a numerical representation used to predict what comes next.

### 2. Zoom into the output: meet the logits
The video zooms into ten output nodes, labeled **A–J**. These represent a simplified vocabulary of ten possible next tokens.

The numbers below them are **logits: raw scores before they become probabilities**.

For example:

- Token A: **3.0**
- Token B: **2.5**
- Token J: **−1.5**
A higher logit means the model favors that token more.

These scores come from combining the hidden-state vector with the output layer’s learned weights:

**Logits = output weights × hidden-state vector + optional bias**

Temperature does **not** change those learned weights.

### 3. What does a negative logit mean?
A negative logit does **not** mean a negative probability or an impossible token.

What matters is the score **relative to the other scores**.

For example, these two sets produce exactly the same softmax probabilities:

**[3, 2, −1]**
**[−2, −3, −6]**

The second set simply subtracts five from every score. Their differences—and therefore their probabilities—remain unchanged.

### 4. Temperature divides the logits
Watch the temperature control on the right side of the video.

The operation is:

**Scaled logit = original logit ÷ temperature**

At **T = 1.00**, the logits remain unchanged.

At **T = 0.50**:

- A: 3.0 ÷ 0.50 = **6.0**
- B: 2.5 ÷ 0.50 = **5.0**
- J: −1.5 ÷ 0.50 = **−3.0**
Notice that the original scores stay fixed in the upper row. Only the scaled scores change.

**Lower temperature widens the gaps between scores.**

The gap between A and B increases from **0.5 to 1.0**. Negative scores become more negative, so it is more accurate to say that the gaps widen than that “all scores increase.”

### 5. Softmax turns scores into probabilities
The scaled scores then pass through **softmax**.

Softmax takes the exponential of each scaled score and divides it by the sum of all those exponentials:

**P(token i) = exp(zᵢ / T) ÷ Σ exp(zⱼ / T)**

You don’t need to memorize the formula. The important idea is:

**Softmax converts scores into positive probabilities that add up to 100%.**

Because it uses exponentials, widening the score gaps gives the highest-scoring token a larger share of the probability.

That is why bar A grows as temperature falls in the video.

The animation keeps the same original logits throughout, so you can isolate the effect of temperature.

### 7. What does the green outline mean?
The green outline shows **top-p sampling**, fixed at **0.90** throughout the video.

Top-p keeps the smallest group of highest-probability tokens whose combined probability reaches or exceeds the chosen threshold.

For **top-p = 0.90**, we add probabilities from the highest downward until the total reaches at least 90%.

In this example:

- **T = 1.00:** A–E are needed. Their combined probability is **92.4%** → **5 tokens**.
- **T = 0.50:** A–C are enough. Their combined probability is **95.0%** → **3 tokens**.
- **T = 0.20:** A alone has **91.8%** → **1 token**.
That explains the shrinking green outline:

**5 → 3 → 1 eligible tokens**

The total can exceed 90% because we include whole tokens, not fractions of a token.

The chart shows probabilities **before top-p filtering**. During sampling, the retained tokens’ probabilities are renormalized to sum to 100%.

### 8. Does lower temperature make the answer deterministic?
**Lower temperature generally makes sampling more predictable, but a positive temperature alone does not guarantee the same choice every time.**

In this particular example, once top-p retains only A, sampling must select A for that prediction step.

Temperature zero is commonly handled as **greedy decoding**: select the highest-scoring token directly. It is not implemented by literally dividing by zero.

The animation stops at **0.05** to show the distribution approaching that behavior.

### 9. What about higher temperature and top-k?
**Higher temperature** narrows the scaled-score gaps, creating a flatter probability distribution. Lower-ranked tokens receive more probability, allowing more varied choices—but not necessarily better or more accurate ones.

**Top-k** keeps a fixed number of highest-ranked tokens. Unlike top-p, its candidate count does not shrink when temperature decreases, because temperature preserves the ranking.

For example, with **top-k = 5**, the same five highest-ranked tokens remain candidates; temperature changes how strongly sampling favors the leader.

### The idea to remember
**Temperature controls how concentrated the probabilities are. Top-p controls which tokens remain eligible for sampling.**

In the video, lowering temperature makes one token increasingly dominant, so fewer tokens are needed to reach the same 90% threshold.

This process repeats at each generation step as the context changes. Temperature is a sampling control—not a measure of the model’s knowledge, intelligence, or factual accuracy.
